MASTER'S PROJECT THESIS

Automated Cybersecurity Assurance Level (CAL) Inheritance

Exploring the automation of Cybersecurity Assurance Level propagation and traceability across automotive development artifacts using CodeBeamer ALM.

CodeBeamer ALM Automotive Cybersecurity TARA ASPICE V-Model Traceability
THE PROBLEM

Keeping cybersecurity assurance information consistent

Cybersecurity Assurance Levels need to remain consistent as cybersecurity requirements move through different stages of automotive development. When this information is tracked manually across interconnected artifacts, maintaining consistency and traceability becomes increasingly difficult.

This project investigated how CAL information could be propagated automatically within CodeBeamer ALM, reducing repeated manual updates while maintaining the relationship between upstream requirements and their downstream engineering artifacts.

APPROACH

Connecting CAL information through the development chain

The approach used the relationships already present between engineering artifacts in CodeBeamer. A CAL value derived from cybersecurity analysis could then be carried through linked development artifacts instead of being repeatedly entered manually.

IMPLEMENTATION

Automation inside CodeBeamer

A dedicated CAL field was introduced at the stakeholder-requirement level. Reference relationships between artifacts were then used to connect the CAL information with downstream development items.

Computed fields and CodeBeamer logic were used to retrieve and propagate the relevant CAL value through the connected development chain. This created a traceable relationship between the original cybersecurity assessment and the corresponding engineering artifacts.

Custom CAL Fields Computed Fields Reference Links Traceability
RESULT

Reducing manual effort while preserving traceability

The implemented workflow demonstrated how CAL information could be propagated across connected development artifacts within CodeBeamer. Changes to the originating CAL information could therefore be reflected through the linked development chain instead of requiring the value to be maintained independently at every level.

The result was a more consistent and traceable approach to handling cybersecurity assurance information across the development process.

SYSTEM DEVELOPMENT

From proof of concept to a 6-DOF robotic test platform

The work progressed in two stages. A Braccio robotic arm was first used as a proof of concept to validate the closed-loop test architecture and investigate reliable interaction with the capacitive sensor.

The concept was then extended to an industrial-grade 6-DOF Arctos robotic platform, providing greater reach, torque, repeatability and flexibility for automated door-handle validation.

CLOSED-LOOP VALIDATION

Connecting robotic motion with ECU feedback

The Braccio proof of concept was used to establish the communication and feedback architecture before moving to the larger robotic platform.

The robotic arm performed the programmed approach or handle interaction while CANoe monitored the corresponding ECU signals. CAPL logic evaluated the sensor response within a defined time window and returned a DETECTED or NOT DETECTED result to the controller.

When detection was not achieved, the controller could retry the action automatically. This created a closed-loop sequence rather than relying only on predefined robot movement.

END-EFFECTOR INVESTIGATION

The robot could reach the handle. The harder problem was making it feel like a finger.

A robotic arm can reproduce position and motion, but a capacitive sensor responds to the electrical interaction between the probe, sensor and surrounding environment. A simple metal tip therefore did not reliably reproduce the behaviour of human interaction.

I investigated different probe materials, geometries and grounding configurations using the Braccio platform. The experiments considered approach and lock interactions at room temperature as well as at approximately -40°C and +80°C.

Force, probe surface area, grounding and probe weight were treated as relevant factors because they influenced the observed sensor response.

01

Copper block

Produced a measurable response when grounded and provided a useful reference for comparison with other probe configurations.

Grounded approach Δ ≈ 60–125
02

Copper plate + NFC antenna + foam

Showed the most consistent response among the investigated configurations and provided a closer match to the measured human-finger reference range.

Approach Δ ≈ 90–120 at RT
03

Copper sheet + electrode gel

Increasing the effective electrode area produced a strong response. Further investigation was required to understand its behaviour under extreme-temperature conditions.

Grounded approach Δ ≈ 95–105
ENGINEERING OBSERVATION

The investigation showed that reliable capacitive interaction was not determined by the material alone. Geometry, surface area, grounding, applied force and environmental conditions all affected the sensor response.

ROBOTIC INTEGRATION

Building the Arctos platform

After validating the core concept, the system was extended to a 6-DOF Arctos robotic arm. The platform provided a more rigid and scalable mechanical architecture for repetitive testing.

My work covered component sourcing, BOM management, mechanical assembly, motor and gearbox installation, encoder positioning, electrical integration and joint calibration.

The system used integrated magnetic encoders and CAN-based communication, allowing the physical robot to be connected to the existing control and validation environment.

ENGINEERING TAKEAWAY

Automation was not only about making the robot move.

The central challenge was making the complete test system behave predictably: mechanical motion, sensor interaction, embedded control, communication and ECU feedback all had to work together.

The project gave me practical experience in taking a robotic system from component-level assembly through commissioning, integration, debugging and validation.

Project-specific product information, internal test documentation and confidential company material have been intentionally excluded from this public case study.

ENGINEERING TAKEAWAY

Turning cybersecurity decisions into traceable engineering data

The project showed how ALM tooling can be used not only to manage engineering information, but also to connect cybersecurity decisions with the development artifacts that depend on them.

For me, the main engineering challenge was not simply storing a CAL value, but understanding how that information moves through the development structure and how the tool can preserve that relationship automatically.
