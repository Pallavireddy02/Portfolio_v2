MASTER'S THESIS · TEST ENGINEERING

Robotic Test Automation for Automotive Door-Handle ECUs

Analysis and improvement of the Lock/Unlock test automation strategy at Minebea AccessSolutions (Oct 2025 – May 2026): an analysis of the existing tester, a closed-loop proof of concept on a Braccio arm, a probe-material study from −40 °C to +80 °C, and the build of a 3D-printed 6-axis Arctos arm for the next test bench.

Test Automation CAN / CANoe CAPL Embedded C/C++ Robotic Arm Assembly Capacitive Sensing
STARTING POINT

Analysing the existing Lock/Unlock tester

The department tested door-handle ECUs on a 3-axis automation tester built on a LabVIEW runtime, an NI DAQ module and PCAN, communicating with the handle over CAN, LIN and K-Line.

Working through the setup showed four limits: only three axes of movement, complex handle-specific fixtures, unreliable triggering of the capacitive sensors, and little room to scale to new handle variants. The thesis set out to keep the existing test know-how while replacing the fixed axes with a robotic arm.

THE ENGINEERING CHALLENGE

Moving the arm was not enough

A robotic arm can repeat a movement, but a test also has to know whether the ECU detected that movement. The arm's action therefore had to be linked to the ECU's response, so the test sequence could react to the result instead of running blindly.

01Arm performs the action
02CANoe monitors the ECU
03CAPL evaluates detection
04Result returns to the arm
PHASE 01 · PROOF OF CONCEPT

A closed-loop test around the Braccio arm

The Braccio arm has no position feedback of its own, so the feedback path was built around it. I wrote C/C++ firmware for an Arduino GIGA R1 with a TJA1051 CAN transceiver, and added commands to the existing CAPL library for the handle.

CAN sync frames open a time window in which CAPL evaluates the raw capacitive values from the ECU. CANoe then sends DETECTED or NOT DETECTED back to the controller, which repeats the action up to two more times if the ECU did not register it.

RESULT

Approach and Unlock ran automatically with feedback from the ECU, and a repeatability and reproducibility (R&R) study was completed. Lock automation was left for the next phase.

END-EFFECTOR STUDY

The arm could reach the handle. The harder part was making it feel like a finger.

A capacitive sensor reacts to the electrical coupling between probe, sensor and surroundings, so a plain metal tip did not behave like a human finger. I compared six probe configurations at 1–5 N contact force, at room temperature, −40 °C and +80 °C, grounded and ungrounded, against reference measurements taken with a human index finger.

OBSERVATION

Material alone did not decide the result. Contact area, grounding, force and temperature all changed the sensor response.

Measured values and the full dataset are internal to the company and are not published here.

PHASE 02 · NEXT TEST BENCH

Building a 3D-printed 6-axis Arctos arm

The next bench is based on the Arctos, an open-source 6-axis arm with closed-loop stepper drives and CAN communication. I sourced the kit against its bill of materials and assembled it: drive train, MKS servo drives, magnetic encoders, limit switches, wiring, and a CANable adapter to the Arctos Studio software.

High-load printed parts were reprinted with more walls and 0.15 mm layers. The planetary gears on two axes bound under load; reprinting them at 96 % scale solved it.

ENGINEERING TAKEAWAY

Turning robotic motion into feedback-driven testing

The useful result was not the arm itself but a test in which the arm, the ECU and the test logic close the loop. Proving that on a small arm first made it clear what the larger bench needs: a probe that behaves like a finger, and a controller that listens to the ECU.
